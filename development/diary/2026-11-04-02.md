# 2026-11-04 (02): an edit is anywhere (start, middle or end), and CoreText line breaking is superlinear in paragraph length

Continues the entry (01), which reached its line ceiling; its claims are cited as "claim N" below. The owner's reminder: an edit does not always happen at the end of a file or of the content, it can be in the middle or at the start.
Everything is a scratch micro-benchmark (release, not kept; load average 50 to 85, so p50 only), written to put a number on what the design had assumed.

## Claim 16: the cost that depends on the position of the edit is small, and the way the tape is shifted decides it

What is position-independent already: reparsing everything and the block diff (claim 10: an edit mid-document and a paragraph inserted at the top cost the same) and a cache keyed by content hash (a block that moves still hits).
What depends on position in the planned incremental edit (0203, phase B): everything after the edit moves. A stand-in for the tapes of a 1 MB document (11,787 block slots and 46,903 inline slots, packed like `TapeSlot`: tag 4 bits, aux 28, low 32), with three shift classes (a source byte offset, a block-tape index, an inline-tape index) chosen by tag and node kind:

| Operation, 1 MB document | At the start | In the middle | At the end |
|---|---|---|---|
| shift both tapes, class table looked up per slot | **88 µs** (1.5 ns per slot) | half | about 0 |
| shift both tapes, a `switch` per slot | 459 µs (7.8 ns per slot) | half | about 0 |
| splice 3 slots by 5 in the inline tape | 21 µs | 9 µs | about 0 |
| insert one byte in the 1 MB source | 62 µs | 32 µs | about 0 |

- **The worst case is an edit at the start (the whole document is the tail) and it costs about 0.18 ms per megabyte; at the end it costs nothing.** That is under the 4 ms ceiling to 20 MB or so; the table-driven shift is five times faster than a `switch`, so the estimate of 5 ns per slot made in the plan was pessimistic for the table and right for the switch.
- Not measured: the real tapes (this is the shape of the loop, not AemiMarkdown's code); the index arrays (hashes, offsets), which are a few thousand integers.

## Claim 17: CoreText's line breaking is superlinear in the length of the paragraph it is given

Probe: `CTTypesetterSuggestLineBreak` and `CTTypesetterCreateLine` over one paragraph of plain text, 13 pt system font, 416 pt wide, every line laid out in order.

| Paragraph | Break every line, one typesetter | Same, in windows of 2000 characters |
|---|---|---|
| 3,000 characters | 1.6 ms | 2.7 ms |
| 10,000 | 71 ms | 14 ms |
| 30,000 | **582 ms** | 96 ms |
| 100,000 | **6.6 s** | 380 ms |

- **The cost is in `CTTypesetterSuggestLineBreak` itself** (the line creation is the small part), and **one call costs O(the whole paragraph)**: three calls from the middle of a 100 KB paragraph took 6.9 ms with a typesetter over the whole text, against 0.5 ms with a typesetter over a 600-character window. So the layout of a paragraph is quadratic, a 30 KB paragraph hangs a viewer for half a second and a 100 KB one for 6.6 seconds.
- **Windowing removes it** (the typesetter sees a bounded stretch of text at a time), and it is also what makes an edit in the middle of a long paragraph cheap: only the windows from the edit until the line breaks line up again with the old ones need laying out again.
- The window size is a tuning question (small windows cost less per call and more per paragraph); 2000 characters was still superlinear inside the window, so the sensible range is a few hundred to a thousand characters. Not tuned here.
- The terminal renderer is not affected: its wrapping is linear (claim 15: 1.14 ms per delta at 100 KB, O(paragraph) per delta, not per call).
- The input that triggers it is ordinary: a paragraph of 3,000 characters costs 1.6 ms, one of 30,000 (a minified blob on one line, a pasted log) half a second. A hostile document is a one-line file.

## Claim 18: public documentation is bigger than what is on this machine, and the parse baseline on it

Setup: AemiMarkdown's benchmark corpus is fetched, not committed: a manifest of public URLs (the documentation sites of Cloudflare, Claude Code, the Claude platform and Codex, which publish Markdown by appending `.md` and an `llms.txt` index, plus changelogs, READMEs and book chapters of public repositories), and a setup step that downloads exactly those into a cache outside the repository and checks each against its SHA-256. No private document is in it.
**A fresh cache was built from the manifest: 998 documents, 84.5 MB, 0 differing from the manifest, 0 unavailable.** The benchmark reads the thread's CPU time, instructions and cycles, which do not depend on what the machine is doing (load average 74 during the last run), and keeps the least of several rounds.

- **Public documents are larger than this machine's own**: median 9.2 KB against 3.2 KB (claim 13), p99 585 KB against 54 KB, 14 of 998 over 400 KB, and one concatenation of a whole site at 49 MB. ## Claim 19: what the block index costs the parse (ask roadmap 0203, first slice)

Change: every top-level block records its source range and an XXH64 content hash as it closes, on a tape of three slots per block committed with the block tape (`MarkdownBlock.sourceRange`, `contentHash`, `ordinal`; `blockCount`, `block(at:)`, `blocks(from:)`, `blockOrdinal(containing:)`).
Correctness first: new tests, **seen failing (67 issues) before the fixes**: the HTML blocks that end on their own line had no range, a table took the line of the paragraph above it, trailing white space of a code line was cut. Now every one of the 682 specification examples and the 998 corpus documents give blocks that parse alone to one block of the same kind and the same hash, with ordered, disjoint, non-empty ranges, and one-byte streaming and snapshots agree. The whole suite passes in release (85 tests in 16 suites, the allocation ceilings included).
Cost, same corpus and harness, instructions per byte before and after: 2 to 5 KB 70.6 to 77.7; 5 to 20 KB 67.3 to 73.9; 20 to 100 KB 64.1 to 69.7; 100 to 400 KB 72.4 to 77.8; 400 KB to 2 MB 76.4 to 81.6; 2 MB and over 94.5 to 102.1: **about +9% instructions and −8% throughput**.
Profile (sampling, documents up to 200 KB): the first version spent 3.5% of the thread finding where a block's last line with text ends (a backward scan per block); a check of the byte before the line ending (almost always text) was added after that profile and is not profiled again. The hash is 1.9% of the thread, the record 0.6%; the rest of the cost is bookkeeping per line that no function accounts for, and is left for the performance program (0205: fuse the hash with the scan, pack the open-block state).

## Claim 20: the differential test found that a block's bytes do not say what it is, and where an edit reaches back

Change: a block's content hash now folds in what its reference links resolved to (a stamp per leaf block that looked a label up, in slot order); it costs about 2% more instructions per byte on the corpus (77.7 to 79.8, 73.9 to 75.4 instructions per byte in the 2 to 5 KB and 5 to 20 KB bins).
Found by testing every specification example at every position with 13 kinds of insertion, and with deletions and replacements (about 300,000 edits, each compared tape for tape with a fresh parse), plus random edits on a document of every kind:
- **The range of a block was not enough to name it.** An unclosed fence, and an HTML block that nothing ended, own the blank lines after their text (they are lines of that code); the range had cut them, so an edit that added a blank line changed the document and not the hash. The range now runs to the end of the input for those two, and the tests that parse a block's own bytes alone pass on all 682 examples.
- **A line can change the block above it.** A table's delimiter row takes the table's header out of the paragraph above, so an edit to the delimiter row changes two blocks, and a restart at the edited block parses a table without its paragraph. The restart now begins at the block above when the edit is on a block's first two lines. The TLA+ model `EditResync` states that rule (with `Variant = "noBack"` it fails; the random test had found it first, at step 138).

## Claim 21: an edit by restart and resync against an edit by parsing the whole (public corpus joined to size, one byte put in a paragraph and taken out, CPU time of the thread and instructions, least of 9 rounds; load average 3 to 16)

| Document | Edit at | Whole: CPU µs / instructions | Restart and resync: CPU µs / instructions |
|---|---|---|---|
| 23 KB, 116 blocks | any | 96 / 1.78 M | 4.5 to 6.4 / 0.07 to 0.12 M |
| 103 KB, 302 blocks | start | 442 / 8.1 M | 11.6 / 0.20 M |
| 1 MB, 2,847 blocks | start | 4,086 / 72.9 M | 57 / 1.08 M |
| 1 MB | last block | 4,079 / 72.9 M | 6.8 / 0.11 M |
| 5 MB, 17,192 blocks | start | 21,660 / 367 M | 252 / 4.84 M |
| 5 MB | middle | 21,663 / 367 M | 172 / 3.82 M |
| 5 MB | last block | 21,636 / 367 M | 5.6 / 0.09 M |

- The whole parse does not depend on the position; the other costs what is after the edit (the move of the tapes and the shift of their offsets) and what the edit region holds, so the start of a 5 MB document is the worst case, 76 times fewer instructions than the whole parse, and the end is 4,000 times fewer.
- An edit in the middle of a document of 5,000 paragraphs reads a block or two (a test counts the slots the region wrote: fewer than 40 block slots, 100 inline slots) wherever it is.
- It parses the whole when a definition came, went or moved inside the region it reads, when the document has a diagnostic, or when the size limit applies: 7 to 30% of random edits whose alphabet is full of `[`, `]` and `:`, and none of an ordinary word typed into a paragraph. Both ways run in the same differential tests against the same oracle.
- After removing the allocations the editor made per edit (a heap table per shift, copies of the arrays it was emptying through a copy of itself, an array literal in the check for `]:`), a warm editor allocates nothing for an edit in a paragraph and about 7 for an edit in a list or a quote (the look at the next block copies a little of the reader's state when something is open): the table above is from before, and the same edits now cost 5 MB start 3.68 M instructions (244 µs), 5 MB last block 0.07 M (4.8 µs), 1 MB start 0.80 M (52 µs). A test holds the ceilings.
- One outlier: at 103 KB, three quarters, the edit costs 0.85 M instructions: the region there is a large block.

## Claim 22: CPU time is not as insensitive to the machine as instructions are

The corpus parse measured 70 to 105 MB/s of CPU time at load average 74 (claim 18) and 145 to 228 MB/s at load average 3 later the same day, with the same instructions per byte (71 to 84). So the instruction count is the number to compare between runs on this machine, and CPU time is only to be compared within one run (a loaded machine shares caches and frequency even when the thread's own clock is ticking). The earlier throughput figures of claim 18 are therefore a floor.

## Claim 23: the performance program, first round (ask roadmap 0205): what was kept and what was reverted

Method: the public corpus, instructions and cycles of the thread, old and new binaries run alternately three or four times and the median taken (the machine was not quiet: load average 10 to 16), the line-level profile from a recorded Time Profiler trace with inlined frames resolved by address. Each change passes the whole suite.

| Experiment | Before to after (instructions per byte, 2 to 5 KB / 5 to 20 KB / 20 to 100 KB / 100 to 400 KB) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| E2 table cells: the cell's end found by a SIMD scan for `\|` and `\` (the loop it replaced is the oracle of a test over every row of up to 7 bytes over 5 symbols, and 20,000 random rows of up to 150 bytes) | 81.1 to 79.2 / 75.8 to 73.0 / 71.3 to 67.8 / 79.7 to 76.7 | **kept** (-2 to -5%, cycles -1.8 to -3.9%) |
| E9 the hash: wyhash (Aemi's `WyHash`) in place of XXH64 for the block hash and the stamps | -0.5% / -0.6% / -0.5% / -0.4% (cycles -1.4 to -1.6%) | **kept** (small; the smaller of two hashes of the same strength, and one function for the family) |
| Labels: a reference lookup needs a normalized label only when the document has a definition, and text without case is not folded through three `String` passes (a profile showed 5.4% of the thread in that line, on documents with letters outside ASCII in brackets); the stamp hashes the label as written | -0.0% / -1.6% / -5.3% / -0.1% (cycles -0.8, -4.9, -6.4%) | **kept**; a test compares the labels of 23 scripts with the three-pass oracle |
| E11 (first step): read the fields of an open block in place instead of copying the 160-byte `Open` for each open block of each line | 81.0 to 81.1 | **reverted**: the compiler had already done it |
| Reading `source` through a local copy in the hot cursor loop (the profile had shown out-of-line array getters) | 81.0 to 81.3 | **reverted**: no change |

All together on the corpus: 81.0 to 78.2 / 75.8 to 71.4 / 71.3 to 63.9 / 79.7 to 76.3 instructions per byte (-3 to -10%), cycles -3.5 to -9%; the larger documents (400 KB and up) gain 2 to 3%.
The profile after these is flat: the largest function is the inline parser's `parse` at 7.7% of the thread, then the SIMD set scan at 5.9%, then the inline tree, array growth and uniqueness checks at 2 to 3% each. What is left is spread over many places, so the remaining experiments (E1 uniqueness checks in the hot arrays, E3 two-stage classification, E4 implicit text runs, E5 lazy inlines, E6 mapped input) are structural, with gains to be shown one by one, and none is expected to be a quick win.

## Claim 24: what laying the public corpus out as rows costs, and the first two changes (ask roadmap 0206)

Probe: `MarkdownRowsProbe` (TerminalKit's Benchmarks package, built against the working tree of AemiMarkdown): every top-level block of each public document up to 400 KB, laid out at 80 columns, CPU time and instructions of the thread, allocations of one pass; load average 15 to 21.

| Size | Rows (sum) | Instructions per source byte, before | after | Allocations per KB, before | after |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 to 5 KB | 62 | 457.6 | 378.4 | 172.1 | 164.6 |
| 5 to 20 KB | 121 | 444.1 | 372.5 | 177.4 | 169.6 |
| 20 to 100 KB | 1,029 | 453.2 | 386.5 | 186.2 | 178.9 |
| 100 to 400 KB | 1,556 | 557.8 | 496.9 | 204.2 | 198.6 |

- **The rows cost about six times the parse** (450 against 70 to 80 instructions per byte) and make **170 to 200 allocations per KB**, against 0.1 for the parse. So the claim 14 picture holds on the public corpus.
- The profile (line level, inlined frames resolved): 12% growing or copying arrays, 5% the width of a string, 3% to 4% each copying the theme for every block (about forty fields), the block writer's and the collector's destructors, and the blank rows.
- Changes kept: the theme is held in one object that a block's writer and collector read, instead of a copy per block; a row outside every container (no quote, no list item) is not built through the prefix machinery. Together -15 to -20% instructions per byte, and, by the probe's CPU time, -55% (the machine was loaded; the instruction counts are the figures to trust). All of TerminalKit's test targets (411 tests in release) pass.
- **Allocations did not move** (-4%): they come from a `String` per run, an array per row, the pieces, the glyphs and the rows of each paragraph, so only the redesign of the roadmap entry (rows as ranges and style ids in an arena) removes them. That is the larger piece of work and it is left open, with the numbers above as its baseline; the prefix of a container is the next place to look (an array per row, a flattened array per blank row).

## Claim 25: AemiMermaid, first cut: a flowchart and a sequence diagram read, laid out and drawn in cells, and drawn in ask's transcript (ask roadmap 0196 to 0198)

Built, in a new repository `AemiMermaid` beside AemiMarkdown (targets `AemiMermaidSyntax`, `Scene`, `Layout`, `Text`, and the `aemi-mermaid` command; no dependency but Aemi's `AemiInterner`; 74 tests):
- **Syntax:** flowchart (the headers and directions, 14 shapes, 13 link forms with both label forms, chains, `&` groups, subgraphs, ignored styling lines) and sequence diagram (participants, actors, aliases, the eight arrows, activations, notes, fragments, autonumber, title); limits (source, nodes, edges, label, nesting, messages, line, virtual nodes); at most 16 diagnostics; labels cleaned when read (controls, C1, bidirectional controls, BOM, invalid UTF-8; `<br/>` and entities decoded).
- **Layout:** flowchart in phases on flat arrays (cycle breaking by an iterative depth-first walk, longest-path ranks, virtual nodes, median sweeps with adjacent swaps and a Fenwick crossing count, placement by pooling adjacent violators, greedy lanes, orthogonal routing), a chart too wide turns top down and then wraps its labels; sequence diagram (columns from the widest text between two, rows in event order, fragments as frames).
- **Cell backend:** per-cell line directions by weight, so meeting lines make the right corner, tee or cross; rounded, double, dashed and heavy lines; arrow, circle, cross and async markers; ASCII mode; the text cleaned again as it is written.
- **ask:** a closed fence whose language is `mermaid` is drawn as the diagram in the transcript (`MermaidRows`), with the styles of the theme by role; an open one (streaming), another language, source the engine cannot read, or a width too narrow stays the code block. Nine tests, one of them drawing a streamed message delta by delta against a fresh layout.

Tests that found things on their first run: the **growth test** (instructions of read, layout and draw at 100, 200, 400 and 800 nodes, chains, trees, sparse graphs and clusters; a doubling may cost less than 3.2 times) found a chain whose nodes drifted a cell to the right at each rank (the placement used a half cell where the rest of the layout uses an integer centre), which made the picture quadratic in its size (5.8 times for a doubling); the **fuzz test** (mutated corpus, 4 seeds, 60 mutations each) found a cluster frame wider than the scene (the title is wider than the nodes), a self loop past the last node, and a note to the left of the first column with no room; the **width sweep** (every corpus diagram at every width from 1 to 120) found nothing.
Cost (release, CPU time of the thread, least of 200, the 14 corpus diagrams of 36 to 227 bytes): **26 to 70 µs for read, layout and draw together** (parse 5 to 16, layout 5 to 28, draw 9 to 44), 350,000 to 940,000 instructions, against the 4 ms ceiling.
Open: the SwiftLint and dolly gates of the new repository (function length, tuples, clones between the two parsers), edges to a subgraph, state, class and ER diagrams, pie and Gantt, network simplex ranking, selection copying the diagram's source, and the pins at landing. In ask, with the two path dependencies (AemiMermaid and the working tree of AemiMarkdown) the whole suite passes (3,547 tests) except `BenchmarkDependencyTests`, which expects a revision for AemiMarkdown in the lock and will again once the pins are back.

## Claim 26: the Quick Look app, its viewer kit, the lab, and what the terminal took from them (ask roadmaps 0199, 0201, 0204)

Built in `AemiMarkdown/Apps/MarkdownLook` (a sub-package and an XcodeGen project; no dependency of ours beyond AemiMarkdown, AemiMermaid and AemiLexers):
- **Viewer kit** (`MarkdownLookKit`): a document as HTML in one pass with an explicit stack, mermaid fences as inline SVG, fenced code coloured by AemiLexers (20 languages, 256 KB limit), a table measured as it is written (short columns on one line, long ones down to their longest word, in a box that scrolls), footnotes that link both ways under a rule, one spacing scale applied between siblings, one indent for lists, quotes and alerts, one stroke width. Nothing in a document acts: raw HTML shown as text, only http, https, mailto and relative links, images not loaded, a Content-Security-Policy that allows no request. 51 tests (structure, hostile input, tag balance, mutation fuzz, nesting, settings, the lab); every public corpus document as a page is a balanced page without script.
- **App and extensions:** a viewer window on SwiftUI's own `WebView` (JavaScript off, subresources off, links to the browser, a soft scroll edge under the title bar, a solid, blurred or clear backdrop), a data-based Quick Look preview and a thumbnail extension (CoreText and CoreGraphics; a diagram or a pie chart for `.mmd`). Eleven settings in one preferences domain that the preview reads through a read-only sandbox exception. The window was blank until the network-client entitlement was added: WebKit's networking process does not start without it.
- **The lab:** a text view reports each change as a byte range, `MarkdownEditor` re-reads the blocks around it, and the page is made from what the editor holds. 300 random edits (insertions of Markdown fragments, multi-byte characters, deletions) each gave the page of a fresh parse of the text. One typed letter in the sample showed 1 block out, 1 in, 81 µs, region only (a single reading on a loaded machine; the instruction counts of claim 16 are the measure).
- **Layout, found by looking at the renders:** edges leaving a node, and edges arriving at one, each get a column of their own (two edges into one state were one thick arrow); a label is against its own line and moves to the other side when it would sit on another edge; boxes are an odd number of cells along the order axis so edges leave from their middle; members of a cluster are grouped in every rank, sibling frames pushed apart, and the channel between ranks leaves room for frame borders (frames had run through each other and over titles). Pie geometry is tested: every wedge starts at the centre, ends on the circle and follows the one before; a report that wedges did not point at the centre could not be reproduced. 102 tests, `swift-ci local` clean.
- **Terminal:** a task item of a bulleted list draws its checkbox where the bullet is, so the text of every item starts in one column; `startsFootnotes` and `footnoteRule` let a caller put a rule above a run of notes (not in a block's own rows: those are cached by content, and a block's neighbour is not in it). ask takes both once its TerminalKit pin moves; its screen composer is unchanged.
- **A test that wiped the reader's settings:** the settings test read and cleared keys in the app's real preferences domain. It now reads a domain of its own.

Not done: the macOS 26 runtime; the Quick Look preview with the final settings and backdrop (the window was seen, the extension was last seen before them); the `.md` thumbnail (the system's own wins on the test machine); a native CoreGraphics view; selection that copies Markdown source; Gantt, mindmap and the other diagram types.
