# 2026-11-04 (01): Mermaid and Markdown viewers, probed: what exists, how it fails, and what Quick Look is today

Research for the Mermaid engine, the SwiftUI viewer and the Quick Look extension (the research note of the same month;
roadmap 0196 to 0202). Everything was run in a scratch directory (not kept) against shallow clones taken today. The machine was
shared with other builds: load average 25 to 225 while the tools built, 25 to 100 while they ran. **Every time below is a median of 3 to 15 runs of
a loaded machine, not a budget.** CLI tools are timed as a whole process (about 10 to 27 ms of it is the process and its fonts); Swift libraries
in process. The structural findings (what fails, what scales how) do not depend on the load; the milliseconds do.

Corpus: 14 hand-written diagrams (flowchart small, left-right with a subgraph, a cycle, CJK and long labels, unterminated, sequence, class,
state, ER, pie, Gantt, a hostile one) and four generated ones: a 200-node and a 1000-node flowchart, 3000 nested subgraphs, a 2 MB label.

## Claim 1: a Mermaid label cannot reach the terminal as a control sequence

Probe: a flowchart whose labels hold a real ESC `]52;c;...` BEL (OSC 52) and ESC `[2J`; count ESC bytes in the output. (The first version of
the file held the text `\u001b`, not the byte, and would have passed everything; it was rebuilt with printf and the counts below are from it.)
**False, in all six.** ESC bytes in the output: mermaid-ascii 2, mmdflux 2, MermaidKit 2, BeautifulMermaid 1, mermaid-rs-renderer 2 (and a BEL) in
its SVG, nessielabs 2 (and a BEL) in its SVG. A control byte in an SVG is not valid XML 1.0, so a viewer may refuse the document, not only a
terminal obey it. Consequence: sanitising is the engine's, at parse, and again where cells are written (0065's sanitiser is not enough).

## Claim 3: the diagram kinds a text renderer covers

Probe: the eight kinds. mermaid-ascii: flowchart and sequence only (a class, state, ER, pie or Gantt diagram is a fatal error). mmdflux: flowchart,

## Claim 4: mermaid-swift (nessielabs) draws like Mermaid

Probe: rendered `flow-lr.mmd` and `seq.mmd` to PNG and looked at them. Subgraph box, cylinder, diamond, edge labels with background, `alt` block

## Claim 5: the dominant way to show Mermaid in a Swift app is Mermaid.js in a web view

## Claim 7: Quick Look today (macOS 27.0.1, Xcode 27.2, SDK `MacOSX27.2.sdk`)

Probes: the SDK's `QuickLookUI` headers, `pluginkit -mAvv -p com.apple.quicklook.preview`, the `Info.plist` of installed extensions, `UTType`.
- The API is unchanged since macOS 12: one extension point, `com.apple.quicklook.preview`; **view-based** (`QLPreviewingController.preparePreviewOfFile(at:completionHandler:)`,
  called once on the main thread, "call the handler as soon as possible", hold no file open) or **data-based** (`QLPreviewProvider` and `QLPreviewReply`: drawn into
  a `CGContext` as bitmap or vector, or PDF through `PDFDocument`, or HTML with `cid:` attachments, or image, plain text, RTF, XML). `QLPreviewReply.h` has no
  newer entry point. Apple's own extensions on this machine use both (Safari's is view-based; OFD's has `QLIsDataBasedPreview`).
- Markdown: `.md` and `.markdown` resolve to `net.daringfireball.markdown`, declared by the system, conforming to `public.plain-text`; `public.markdown` is declared;
  `net.ia.markdown` is not (Obsidian's extension lists all three). `.mdown .mkd .mkdn .mdwn .mmd .mermaid .qmd .mdx` are dynamic types (unclaimed).
- The system previews Markdown as plain text (`QLPreviewGenerationExtension`: `public.plain-text`); the only Markdown extension installed here is Obsidian's.
- Thumbnails are a separate extension point (`QuickLookThumbnailing`, `QLThumbnailProvider`); none is installed for Markdown here.
- The family builds extensions with XcodeGen over SwiftPM packages: MacFS's XcodeGen spec (deployment target 27.0, `projectFormat: xcode16_3`, strict flags as build settings).

## Claim 8: what the system font does, so the typography can lean on it

Probe: `NSFont` metrics and `NSAttributedString.size()` in a scratch Swift program (release, macOS 27.0.1), sizes 11 to 48 pt.
- **The system font's own line height is 1.178 em at every size, with zero leading** (ascender 0.967, descender 0.211 em at 17 pt: 16.44 and 3.59). That is too tight for prose,
  so a viewer must set the line height itself. Cap height 0.705 em, x-height 0.526 em (17 pt: 11.98 and 8.95).
- **Tracking and optical size are automatic**: the same text is 11% narrower per em at 48 pt than at 11 pt (8.18 to 7.29 em for one word; 16.65 to 14.80 for two). So never add manual tracking
  to the system font, and **draw text at its final point size**: scaling a drawing by a transform keeps the 11 pt shapes and spacing.
- **Weight changes the width**: 28 pt "Hamburgefonstiv" is 7.39 em regular, 7.65 medium, 7.84 semibold, 8.11 bold (up to 10% wider), so a heading is measured and wrapped at its real weight.
- **Kerning is on by default** and worth about 7% on "AVATAR To Ty" (105.2 against 112.9 with `.kern 0`): never switch it off in prose.
- **Digits in the system font are proportional**: "1111" is 29.8 pt and "0000" 41.1 at 17 pt; `monospacedDigitSystemFont` makes both 41.1. Numbers in columns need the tabular variant.
- **The system monospaced font has the same x-height as the proportional one at the same size** (6.87 against 6.84 at 13 pt, 7.93 against 7.90 at 15 pt), advance 0.618 em, and **no ligatures** (`=> -> != ===` measures 12 cells with or without `.ligature`). The usual advice to set code at 85 to 90% of the body size is for fonts that do not match; here 100% matches.
- **Average advance of English prose is 0.448 em per character** (232 characters, spaces included), so a measure of 66 to 75 characters is 30 to 34 em.
- **macOS text styles** (`preferredFont(forTextStyle:)`): large title 26, title1 22, title2 17, title3 15, body 13, headline 13 bold, callout 12, subheadline 11, footnote 10, caption 10. Body is a UI size, 13 pt.
- Design variants of the system font exist: serif resolves to New York, rounded and monospaced to system variants (names `.NewYork-Regular`, `.AppleSystemUIFontRounded-Regular`, `.AppleSystemUIFontMonospaced-Regular`). No font is named or bundled.
Not probed: how these render at small sizes on a 1x display, and what `NSTextView` (TextKit 2) does with `lineBreakStrategy` and hyphenation on long headings.

## Claim 9: what Apple recommends as a default reading size

Probe: the HIG typography page (apple-docs mirror) and AppKit constants and line layout, macOS 27.0.1.
- HIG text (read): follow "the recommended default and minimum text sizes for each platform", prefer the built-in text styles, avoid light weights, use `Font.Design` and never embed system fonts, and for "wide columns or long passages" use looser leading;
  **macOS does not support Dynamic Type**; "in a running app, the system font dynamically adjusts tracking at every point size". The size and tracking tables are images and did not survive the mirror, so **the numbers are not quoted from the HIG**.
- AppKit (probed): `NSFont.systemFontSize` 13, `smallSystemFontSize` 11, `labelFontSize` 10; the built-in text styles range from 26 down to 10 (claim 8). A TextKit 1 line of the 13 pt system font is **16.0 pt, 1.23 em** (the font's own metrics are 15.31, 1.178 em), so Apple's body is 13 pt on a 16 pt line.
- The `looseLeading` and `tightLeading` font traits **change nothing** in `NSFont`'s metrics (identical ascender, descender, leading at 13, 15 and 17 pt): the loosening happens in SwiftUI or UIKit layout, so a viewer that wants looser leading sets the line height itself.
Verdict: the recommended reading default on macOS is the body style, 13 pt, 1.23 em line, loosened for long passages by the app; iOS takes it from Dynamic Type at run time (its default of 17 pt is from memory, not probed here).

## Claim 10: an edit can reach the screen's rows in under 10 ms, today, up to about 400 KB

The owner's next requirement: edit a Markdown file and see the render at the same time, sub-10 ms, by block, in SwiftUI and in the terminal. Probe: a scratch package (release, macOS 26 target) over AemiMarkdown `9dc938e`
and TerminalKitMarkdown, on real Markdown (ask's roadmap, research, ADR and diary files, concatenated): parse the whole document again after an edit, find which top-level blocks changed by hashing each block's events (what a consumer must do today:
no block source range exists, ADR-0002), and lay out only those blocks as terminal rows at width 100. Two edits: one character typed mid-document, and a paragraph inserted at the top (every block id shifts). Two runs, load average 65 to 130, agreeing within 10%.

| Document | Parse | Hash per block | Render the changed block | **Edit to rows, p50 (p99)** | Render every block |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 23 KB, 144 blocks | 0.23 ms | 0.11 ms | 0.02 ms | **0.35 ms** (0.42) | 1.9 ms |
| 103 KB, 569 blocks | 0.96 ms | 0.46 ms | 0.02 ms | **1.4 ms** (1.8) | 7.8 ms |
| 1.0 MB, 3592 blocks | 9.4 to 11.3 ms | 4.0 to 4.7 ms | 0.2 ms | **16 ms** (16.5) | 96 ms |
| 5.0 MB, 17745 blocks | 46 to 55 ms | 16 to 22 ms | 0.4 ms | **64 to 78 ms** (64 to 80) | 350 to 385 ms |

- **Parse speed on documents is about 100 MB/s**, not the 200 MB/s of the README, which was measured on assistant messages; tables, lists and links cost more than prose. Two edits cost the same: reparsing everything does not care where the edit is.
- **Reparse everything plus a changed-block diff is under 10 ms up to roughly 400 KB; at 1 MB it is not (16 ms).** Past that an edit has to cost O(the edit), not O(the document).
- **Rendering is the cost that block caching removes**: laying out one block is 0.02 ms, every block 7.8 ms at 103 KB (400 times more). Parse is 1 ms; so the cache, not the parser, is what makes typing cheap at the sizes people write.
- **The consumer-side block diff is half the parse again** (0.46 against 0.96 ms): a parser that records each block's source range and a hash as the block closes makes it nearly free.

## Claim 11: a single TextKit 2 text view is not a sub-10 ms host for large documents

Probe: `NSTextView` on TextKit 2 in an offscreen window (release, scratch program, one run per size, loaded machine), paragraphs of 30 to 70 words at 13 pt with a 1.4 em line, one edit then a viewport layout, 60 repetitions.

| Document | Type one character in view, p50 (p99) | Replace a whole paragraph in view | Replace one far below the view |
|---|---|---|---|
| 200 paragraphs, 51 K characters | 1.7 ms (3.2) | 1.8 ms (3.0) | 0.8 ms (1.5) |
| 2000 paragraphs, 0.5 M | 3.3 ms (**31**) | 3.5 ms (22) | 1.5 ms (40) |
| 10000 paragraphs, 2.5 M | 5.4 ms (**39**) | 3.3 ms (37) | 1.8 ms (40) |

**The cost grows with the document although one paragraph changed, and the tail is 30 to 40 ms from 0.5 M characters up.** Drawing the viewport (`cacheDisplay`, a software path, not what the screen does) took 4.3 to 15.8 ms and also grew. One run per size: a direction, not a baseline.
Read: a text view that owns the whole document is fine for small ones and the wrong place for a block cache; an own windowed layout is the candidate to measure against it (0199).

## Claim 12: CoreText lays a block out fast enough to do it per keystroke, and the drawing stays vector

Probe (same scratch work): CoreText `CTFramesetter` and frame for a 50-word paragraph at 416 pt, 13 pt system font, 400 paragraphs: **0.16 ms p50, 0.37 ms p99**. Then the drawing was sent to a PDF context and the PDF searched for image objects, with a
control that must fire (a bitmap `NSImage` gives 2 image objects and an `/SMask`, in both a PDF context and SwiftUI's `ImageRenderer`):
- CoreText text and `CGPath` curves: 0 images, 2 fonts. SwiftUI `Text` plus a `Canvas` with a path and `GraphicsContext.draw(Text)`: 0 images, 2 fonts embedded. With `.scaleEffect(4)`: 0 images. A `Canvas` with `rendersAsynchronously`: 0 images.
- An SVG file in `NSImage` (a `_NSSVGImageRep`, a private class behind the public type): 0 images, 0 fonts, paths only, drawn at 8 times its size. So local SVGs in a document stay vector.
- **`.drawingGroup()` is inconclusive**: `ImageRenderer` produced 0 images for it, so it does not exercise the on-screen path; Apple documents it as compositing "into an offscreen image before final display" (read), so it is treated as raster and banned.

## Claim 14: where the time and the memory go, so that every optimization starts from a profile

The owner's rule (2026-11-04): use every optimization technique for the least resources and the fastest parse and render, whatever the file sizes, incremental or complete. Method before technique: profile, name the hot spot, change one thing, measure again.
Probe: the macOS `sample` profiler on a 1 MB document, 5 seconds, release, on the main thread only (idle worker threads excluded; 2987 samples for the parser, 2900 for the terminal renderer). Sampling slows the program several times over, so the **shape** is real and the absolute throughput is not.

| Parser, complete parse | Share of the main thread |
|---|---|
| the SIMD set-scan kernel (`adf_index_of_set`) | 16.1% |
| splitting table cells | 10.0% |
| the inline parser's main routine | 7.1% |
| copy-on-write uniqueness checks on arrays (`isUniquelyReferenced`) | 6.5% |
| memory moves | 5.7% |
| the Swift runtime in total (retain, release, array, bridging) | 10% |
| malloc and free | 1% |
| our own code | 83% |

| Terminal renderer, every block | Share |
|---|---|
| the Swift runtime in total (retain, release, bridge, array copies) | 40% |
| malloc and free | 13% |
| memory moves | 5% |
| our own code | 41% (row wrapping 7.8% + 3.9%, display width 3.5%, grapheme breaking about 3%) |

Read: **the parser is already close to its kernel** (most of the time is our logic and the SIMD scan; the avoidable parts are table cells, copy-on-write checks and moves, about a fifth of the time); **the renderer spends more than half its time in String, reference counting and allocation**,
not in layout, so it is where the largest factor lies (a row of byte ranges and style ids instead of `String` runs). Memory: a 1 MB document gives 11,787 block slots and 46,903 inline slots, **469,520 bytes of tape, 0.47 bytes per source byte**, plus one copy of the source.
Build: the compiler command line of a release build carries `-enable-default-cmo`, so cross-module optimization is already on by default for SwiftPM release builds (read from the build log).

## Claim 15: what a stream delta costs to land, and what drawing costs

The owner (2026-11-04): an edit or a stream should land the render instantaneously, the earlier the better. Probe 1: a scratch package streams real Markdown into AemiMarkdown in 40-byte deltas (a snapshot after each) and draws what a host must: the open block again, each newly final block once, through TerminalKitMarkdown at width 100 (release).
Probe 2: CoreText frames of 50-word paragraphs drawn into a bitmap context of 760 by 900 points at 2x with CoreGraphics. Load average 40 to 70 throughout.

| Stream, per 40-byte delta, p50 | Total | Append | Snapshot | Block iteration | Render open block |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| documents, 100 KB (569 blocks) | **35 µs** | 0.5 | 4.9 | 13.2 | 15.5 |
| documents, 1 MB (3592 blocks) | 138 µs | 0.5 | 14.3 | **84.0** | 24.5 |
| one 20 KB paragraph | **200 µs** | 3.6 | 8.1 | 0.2 | 181 |
| one 100 KB paragraph | **1230 µs** | 15 | 35 | 0.6 | 1140 |

- **A delta of ordinary text lands in tens of microseconds**, and the append itself in half a microsecond.
- **The cost of the open block grows with its length**: 181 µs at 20 KB and 1.14 ms at 100 KB for a paragraph with no break, so streaming such a paragraph is quadratic (100 KB took 13 s). Every delta lays the whole open block out again; only its last line can change.
- **Walking the blocks costs O(blocks) per delta**: the iteration starts at block 0 and there is no way to start from the first non-final block; 13 µs at 569 blocks, 84 µs at 3592.
- **The tail is not our code.** Deltas over 1 ms (20 to 35 ms the worst) appeared in every run, but three identical runs shared none of them (34, 36 and 29 deltas over 2 ms, none in all three, one in two): it is the scheduler on a loaded machine. p99 and max from this machine say nothing about the code; p50 does.

| Drawing, bitmap 760 x 900 pt at 2x | p50 |
|---|---|
| clear the viewport | 0.47 ms |
| draw the visible blocks (about 14 paragraphs, about 700 words) | **6.4 ms** |
| repaint one changed block, clipped to its rectangle | **0.18 ms** |

**A full redraw of the viewport costs more than the whole 4 ms ceiling; repainting only the changed block costs 0.18 ms.** A drawing that is redone in full on every change (a `Canvas` whose state changes, a view that invalidates everything) pays the 6.4 ms; a dirty rectangle does not.
Verdict for "instantaneous": tail-only layout of the open block, block iteration from the first non-final block, and dirty-rectangle repaint are what the numbers ask for (roadmap 0199, 0203, 0206 and the plan's latency section).
